0. Literacy for the information age

Digital literacy is the mix of technical, thinking, and social skills that lets a person find information, organise it, turn it into something useful, and share it responsibly. Access to a device is not enough. The Information Age dumps more pages on you in a day than a 20th-century library did in a month; the scarce skill is judgement.

1. Two kinds of data

Qualitative

Descriptive. Opinions, reasons, observed behaviour. “Students say the LMS login fails during exams.” Helps you understand why.

Quantitative

Numerical. Counts, marks, hours online. “$62\%$ of the class uses the internet more than $4$ hours a day.” Helps you compare and chart.

A decent school project almost always needs both: hours on the phone (quantitative) and the sentence “I check notifications between every paragraph” (qualitative).

4. Designing a data-collection approach

1
Write one clear research question (not a topic). Example: “How does daily mobile-phone use affect study habits of Class XI students in this college?”
2
Define the population and a realistic sample.
3
Choose methods (survey + two interviews is a strong school mix).
4
Draft fair, non-leading questions. Mix closed (numbers) and open (reasons).
5
Pilot on three classmates; fix confusing wording.
6
Ask permission. Do not collect names you do not need. That is research ethics, not decoration.
7
Collect, back up, clean.
8
Analyse and present. Then say what you still do not know.

5. Search like a researcher, not like a tourist

A lazy query dumps blogs. Operators carve the pile.

"internet usage in Pakistan" filetype:pdf
site:.edu  "study habits"  smartphone
"mobile phone use" "study habits"
Table 3. Operators worth memorising
OperatorJob
"exact phrase"Keep those words together
filetype:pdfPrefer reports over slideshows
site:edu or site:gov.pkStay inside a domain family
OR / -Widen / exclude a word

Source checklist: named author or institution? date? citations? evidence or only adjectives? other side mentioned?

6. Presenting without drowning the reader

Spreadsheet
Excel / Google Sheets: tidy columns, formulas, charts. They do not “make the research correct”; they organise what you already collected.
Presentation
One claim per slide, a chart, a short sentence. Not a pasted essay.
Infographic
A single visual argument for a corridor wall or a report cover.
Report / artefact
Question, method, findings, limits, references. The artefact’s job is to communicate results, not to decorate.

7. Integrated case — phone use and study habits

The textbook walks a full mini-project: advanced search, source checklist, a Google/Microsoft Form, Sheets charts, and a digital artefact. Quantitative layer: hours per day, percentage who report distraction. Qualitative layer: why they pick up the phone. Ethics: permission, no unnecessary names, backups.

Ethics & honesty
Copying words, images, or ideas without credit is plagiarism. Copyright is the legal shield; a citation is how you stay on the right side of it. Asking permission before a survey is part of the method, not an afterthought.

Self-check (exam style)

  1. Which query is best for a reliable PDF on internet use in Pakistan? (a) internet usage Pakistan (b) best internet social media (c) "internet usage in Pakistan" filetype:pdf (d) Pakistan internet all websites latest
  2. Survey responses from your own class are ______ data.
  3. Name the error: pasting a blog paragraph into your report with no quote marks and no reference.
Answers. (1) (c). (2) Primary. (3) Plagiarism.

Recap

  1. Digital literacy = find + collect + judge + present + cite.
  2. Qualitative explains; quantitative measures. Use both.
  3. Interviews, surveys, observation, prototypes, simulations.
  4. Primary = you collected it; secondary = you reused it.
  5. A research question focuses the whole design. Permission is ethics.
  6. Search operators: quotes, filetype:, site:.
  7. Spreadsheets, slides, infographics, reports — clarity over decoration. Never plagiarise.
