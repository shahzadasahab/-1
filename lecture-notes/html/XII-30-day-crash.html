30-Day Crash · Day 01Unit 1 · Human–Computer Interaction
DAY01

The HCI loop

Unit 1 · Human–Computer Interaction · 120-minute coached session

10 minRecall, cold
25 minTeach from the board
25 minWork the example together
40 minSilent drill, then mark
20 minHomework set and traps
Leave able to
  • Define HCI in one sentence
  • Name five sensory channels
  • Explain why missing feedback is a defect
Trap that costs the mark

Defining HCI as ‘making screens pretty’. Pretty is later. The loop comes first.

Board notes

HCI (also CHI or MMI) is the design of technology so a person can use it efficiently and without being shut out. UI is what you see. The loop is the subject: a human sends input, the computer answers with feedback, and the human decides what to do next.

Channels: sight (screen), touch (tap, keys, vibration), hearing (voice prompts), voice (spoken commands), spatial (body position in a motion game). A system that changes state and does not tell you has a broken loop, not a minimalist style.

Worked example — do not skip the steps

Board. Student taps “Submit” on an attendance app. Sight: the button darkens. Touch: a short vibration. Hearing: optional click. If none of these happen, the student taps again and you get duplicate rows. Write the repair: a visible “Saved” line plus a disabled button. That is feedback, and it is worth marks.

Silent drill — cover the green model lines

1. HCI in one sentence.

Model. Designing the exchange between a person and a computer so the system is usable, efficient, and respectful of human limits.

2. Name the five channels.

Model. Sight, touch, hearing, voice, spatial.

3. UI or the loop: a button with no confirmation.

Model. The loop is broken. Feedback is missing, so the user cannot tell success from failure.

4. Two other names for HCI.

Model. CHI (computer–human interface) and MMI (man–machine interaction).

5. Why does a coach care about vibration on a phone?

Model. Touch feedback reaches users who cannot rely on the screen alone, and it confirms a tap.
Tonight
Sketch the two circles, human and computer, and label both arrows. No icons required.

Traditional and natural interaction

Unit 1 · Human–Computer Interaction · 120-minute coached session

10 minRecall, cold
25 minTeach from the board
25 minWork the example together
40 minSilent drill, then mark
20 minHomework set and traps
Leave able to
  • Give two examples of each family
  • Name four interaction styles
  • Name three UI families
Trap that costs the mark

Calling every touchscreen ‘natural’. Tap and swipe are traditional phone interaction. Gesture-in-the-air and voice are the natural family.

Ten-minute recall — yesterday, closed book

AskAccept
Five channels?Sight, touch, hearing, voice, spatial
What is missing if a tap does nothing visible?Feedback

Board notes

Traditional interaction uses keyboard, mouse, and tap or swipe. A PC answers on a monitor and speakers. A phone answers with light, sound, and vibration.

Natural interaction uses voice, gesture, face, and spatial tracking: a smart speaker, a wave to dismiss a call, face unlock.

Styles you should be able to list: direct manipulation, command line, menus, forms, conversation, gesture, voice, context-aware, biometric, haptic, multimodal. UI families: GUI, CLI, touch, voice UI, and NUI (natural UI such as VR).

Components of an HCI situation: user (and other stakeholders), computer, task, interface, environment, and feedback in both directions.

Worked example — do not skip the steps

Board: college attendance. Traditional version: teacher taps a row on a phone (touch GUI, form fill). Natural version: a camera recognises a face at the door (biometric, context-aware). Say the trade: natural can be faster and can also fail on lighting, privacy, and consent. The crash answer names the style and one risk, not a gadget catalogue.

Silent drill — cover the green model lines

1. Mouse and keyboard belong to which family?

Model. Traditional interaction.

2. Face unlock belongs to which family?

Model. Natural interaction, biometric.

3. One GUI and one CLI example.

Model. A phone home screen is a GUI. A terminal command such as python file.py is a CLI.

4. What is multimodal?

Model. More than one channel at once, for example captions plus vibration plus a spoken prompt.

5. User versus stakeholder.

Model. The user operates the system. A stakeholder is affected or decides, such as a parent who never opens the attendance app but receives the SMS.

Trap that costs the mark

Saying ‘we used blue so it is accessible’. Colour choice without contrast and without a non-colour cue does not pass.

Ten-minute recall — yesterday, closed book

AskAccept
Traditional input devices?Keyboard, mouse, tap and swipe
Natural examples?Voice, gesture, face, spatial tracking

Board notes

Accessibility is not a theme you add if there is time. Four constraints the paper expects:

ConstraintFailPass
ContrastPale grey on whiteDark text on a light ground, or the reverse
ReadabilityDecorative font, long linesA plain face, a sane size, short lines
Colour-blind safetyRed means error, with no other cueIcon plus the word “Failed”
Inclusive navigationMouse onlyTab order, visible focus, screen-reader names, captions

A greyscale test is the one-minute audit: if the meaning disappears when colour disappears, the interface is not done.

Worked example — do not skip the steps

Board audit of a login. Placeholder text is light grey. The error is a red border only. The submit control is a 16-pixel icon. Repairs, in mark order: darker label text (contrast), the sentence “Password too short” (not colour alone), a larger target with the word Submit (readability and hit size), and a tab order that reaches the button (keyboard).

Silent drill — cover the green model lines

1. Why is red-only an accessibility defect?

Model. Colour-blind users and anyone in greyscale lose the signal. Add a word and an icon.

2. One contrast failure.

Model. Pale grey text on a white card, especially outdoors.

3. What should a keyboard user see?

Model. A visible focus ring and a sensible Tab order, including the primary button.

4. Name one screen reader.

Model. NVDA or VoiceOver.

5. Captions support which channel when hearing is unavailable?

Model. Sight. They move speech into text.
Tonight

AskAccept

Board notes

A need is the gap between the current state and the desired state. Steps: identify users, collect information, analyse, prioritise, propose solutions. Design for a named person, not for “users”.

SymptomLikely causeRepair
Cannot find the next actionWeak hierarchyOne obvious primary button
Error with no explanationSystem-centred textPlain language plus how to fix it

Worked example — do not skip the steps

Board: mobile banking for a grandparent. Current state: a six-field transfer and a 12-point font. Desired state: send money to a saved name without fear. Needs, in priority: large type, a biometric login, a confirmation sentence, a second language. A 19-year-old who only tests on their own phone will miss all four. That sentence is the need-analysis mark.

Silent drill — cover the green model lines

1. Define a need.

Model. The gap between how things are and how they need to be for a named user.

2. Five steps, in order.

Model. Identify users, collect information, analyse, prioritise, propose solutions.

3. Tiny targets. Repair.

Model. Enlarge the controls and separate them so a thumb hits one thing.

4. ‘Invalid input’ as the only error. What is wrong?

Model. It does not say which field failed or how to repair it.

5. Why interview someone who is not you?

Model. Your habits hide the gaps. A different age, language, or eyesight reveals them.

Leave able to
  • Separate UI from UX
  • Sketch a low-fidelity wireframe

Trap that costs the mark

Starting in high fidelity because the tool makes it easy. You will defend colours instead of fixing the flow.

Ten-minute recall — yesterday, closed book

AskAccept
Five need-analysis steps?Users, collect, analyse, prioritise, propose
A need is a what?A gap between current and desired

Board notes

UI is the visual and interactive layout: type, colour, controls, spacing. UX is the whole journey: time, trust, errors, and how you feel when you leave. “The transfer took four taps and I was never unsure” is a UX sentence. The button style that made the fourth tap obvious is UI.

A wireframe is a layout blueprint. A prototype is an early model you can tap before production code.

Low fidelityHigh fidelity
Looks likeBoxes and labelsColour, type, images, motion
ForStructure and flow, cheap changesLook-and-feel, a stakeholder demo
SpeedMinutes, including paperHours or days

Worked example — do not skip the steps

Board, four frames, low fidelity. Welcome, Sign in, Class list, Saved. Each frame is a rectangle. One primary button. No colour. Arrows between frames are the flow. You are not allowed to discuss the logo until a classmate can tap the paper story without asking where to press.

Silent drill — cover the green model lines

1. UI or UX: ‘I trusted the confirmation sentence’.

Model. UX. Trust is the journey, even though UI carried the sentence.

2. When do you stay at low fidelity?

Model. While the flow is still wrong. Changes are cheap. Polish on a bad flow wastes the hour.

3. What is a prototype?

Model. An early, tappable model used to test layout and flow before the final build.

4. Four frames for attendance, names only.

Model. Welcome, Sign in, Mark class, Confirmed. Other names are fine if the story is complete.

5. Why one primary button?

Model. So the next action is unambiguous. Extra buttons hide the path.

Trap that costs the mark

Explaining the interface while the user is stuck. Their pause is the data. Your explanation hides it.

Ten-minute recall — yesterday, closed book

AskAccept
Low fidelity is for what?Structure and flow, changed cheaply
UI versus UX in one line?UI is the layout. UX is the whole journey.

Board notes

Figma is the browser tool for this unit. Crash path: new file, press F for the frame tool, drag a phone frame, duplicate it for each screen, put a title and one button on each, open the Prototype tab, drag a link from the button to the next frame, present, and tap through.

Evaluation: SUS (System Usability Scale, a short score out of 100), NPS (would you recommend this?), and task success (did they finish, in how many seconds, with how many errors). Worked example — do not skip the steps

Board test of the attendance prototype. Task: “Mark Ali present and reach a confirmation.” You say nothing. You write the seconds and the pauses. A pause on the class list is a defect in hierarchy, not a clumsy user. Formative, because you will change the frame tonight. A SUS form after the finished flow would be summative.

Model. F, then drag.

2. Where do you connect a button to the next screen?

Model. The Prototype tab.

3. What does SUS measure, roughly?

Model. Perceived usability, reported as a score out of 100.

4. NPS asks what?

Model. Whether the person would recommend the product.

5. Formative or summative: a paper test in week one.

Model. Formative. You are still changing the design.

DAY08

Correctness by hand

Unit 2 · Algorithms and Data Structures · 120-minute coached session

10 minRecall, cold
25 minTeach from the board
25 minWork the example together
40 minSilent drill, then mark
20 minHomework set and traps
Leave able to
  • Fill a trace table
  • State a loop invariant in words
  • Catch the empty-input case
Trap that costs the mark

A final number with no rows. The rows are the answer.

Ten-minute recall — yesterday, closed book

AskAccept
HCI checkpoint pass mark?7/10
Formative evaluation happens when?During design

Board notes

An algorithm is correct when every valid input produces the specified output and the process stops. Two tools: a trace table (line, variables, condition, output) and stepwise reasoning (an invariant such as “after k steps, S is the sum of the first k integers”).

Trace tables catch arithmetic slips. Invariants catch structural lies, including the empty case $N=0$, which a trace of $N=3$ can miss.

\[ S = \frac{N(N+1)}{2} \]

Worked example — do not skip the steps

Board: sum 1..N for N=3.

LineiSi ≤ 3
start—0—
loop11true
loop23true
loop36true
exit46false

Closed form checks the trace: $3 \times 4 / 2 = 6$. For $N=0$ the loop never runs and $S$ stays 0, which is correct. Say that edge aloud.

Silent drill — cover the green model lines

1. What must a correct algorithm do besides ‘usually work’?

Model. Match the specification for every valid input, and stop.

2. A trace table is best at catching what?

Model. Arithmetic slips, bad updates, and loops that do not end, on a small n.

3. An invariant is best at catching what?

Model. A structural mistake, including forgotten edge cases.

4. Sum 1..3 by the closed form.

Model. 6.

5. Sum 1..0. What should happen?

Model. The loop does not run. The sum stays 0.
Tonight
Trace sum 1..4. The last S must be 10, and you must show the failing condition.

DAY09

Clarity: modules and names

Unit 2 · Algorithms and Data Structures · 120-minute coached session

10 minRecall, cold
25 minTeach from the board
25 minWork the example together
40 minSilent drill, then mark
20 minHomework set and traps
Leave able to
  • Give one function one job
  • Rename a mystery variable
  • Say which refinement is clarity and which is speed
Trap that costs the mark

Commenting every line instead of renaming. A comment that repeats the code is not clarity.

Ten-minute recall — yesterday, closed book

AskAccept
Closed form for sum 1..N?N(N+1)/2
Trace tables show what?Each variable after each step

Board notes

Modularity means one function, one job, loosely coupled. calculate_sum and find_average beat a forty-line blob. Readability means names that carry units, shallow nesting, and comments only where the why is not obvious. velocity = distance / time documents itself.

Refine for clarity by renaming and extracting. Refine for efficiency by doing less work: stop early, avoid recomputing, or pick a better structure. Do not confuse the two. A faster mess is still a mess. A clear $O(n^2)$ on twenty rows is often the right classroom choice.

Worked example — do not skip the steps

Board, before and after. Before: one block reads numbers, sums them, divides, and prints a sentence. After: total(nums) returns the sum, average(nums) returns total divided by length and refuses an empty list, main only prints. The behaviour is the same. The clarity mark is the split, not a new formula.

Silent drill — cover the green model lines

1. Single responsibility in one line.

Model. A function does one job that you can name with one verb.

2. Replace x = a / b with a readable line.

Model. velocity = distance / time, or any names that carry meaning.

3. Clarity refinement or efficiency refinement: extracting a function.

Model. Clarity.

4. Efficiency refinement: one example.

Model. Stop a search when the item is found, or replace a nested scan with a better structure.

5. Why refuse an empty list inside average?

Model. Division by zero. The function should not pretend the mean of nothing is zero.
Tonight
Take any 15-line program you wrote in Class XI and split it into two named functions. Do not add features.

DAY10

Big-O without the folklore

Unit 2 · Algorithms and Data Structures · 120-minute coached session

10 minRecall, cold
25 minTeach from the board
25 minWork the example together
40 minSilent drill, then mark
20 minHomework set and traps
Leave able to
  • Classify constant, log, linear, and quadratic
  • Spot a nested loop
  • Say what short-circuiting saves
Trap that costs the mark

Saying $O(1)$ means ‘fast in real seconds’. It means the step count does not grow with n.

Ten-minute recall — yesterday, closed book

AskAccept
One function, one what?Job / responsibility
A clarity refinement example?A meaningful name or an extracted function

Board notes

Efficiency has two axes: time (steps as $n$ grows) and space (extra memory). The course expects these classes:

ClassGrowthClassroom example
$O(1)$Does not grow with nArray index, hash lookup
$O(\log n)$Halves the workBinary search
$O(n)$One passLinear search, one loop
$O(n \log n)$Good general sortDivide-and-conquer sort (named, not derived)
$O(n^2)$Nested loopsBubble sort, all-pairs compare

Logarithmic is excellent, linear is good, quadratic becomes poor as $n$ leaves the classroom. Short-circuit a compound test when the first clause already decides it.

Worked example — do not skip the steps

Board. One loop over a class list is $O(n)$. A loop inside a loop over the same list is $O(n^2)$. Reading marks[i] is $O(1)$. Binary search on sorted roll numbers is $O(\log n)$. Say the three questions from the book: Does it scale? Where is the bottleneck? Is the cost worth it?

Silent drill — cover the green model lines

1. Nested loops usually mean which class?

Model. $O(n^2)$.

2. Array index access?

Model. $O(1)$.

3. Binary search?

Model. $O(\log n)$, on sorted data.

4. A single pass that prints every name?

Model. $O(n)$.

5. Why short-circuit a and b?

Model. If a is false, b cannot save the expression, so you skip the work.
Tonight
Label four snippets you invent: index, one loop, nested loops, binary search. Four labels, no essays.

Unit 2 · Algorithms and Data Structures · 120-minute coached session

10 minRecall, cold
25 minTeach from the board
25 minWork the example together
40 minSilent drill, then mark
20 minHomework set and traps
Leave able to
  • State the cost of an index
  • Explain a node
  • Choose a list when inserts are frequent
Trap that costs the mark

Drawing a list and then writing A[3]. That notation belongs to the array.

Ten-minute recall — yesterday, closed book

AskAccept
O(n^2) pattern?Nested loops
O(log n) example?Binary search

Board notes

An array is a fixed sequence with indexes, often from 0. Reading A[i] is $O(1)$. Inserting in the middle pushes neighbours and is painful. Classroom chairs with painted numbers.

A linked list is nodes of (data + pointer). You walk from the head. There is no jump to the tenth node. Inserting once you are standing on the node is natural. Browser back/forward history is the picture.

Worked example — do not skip the steps

Board choice. Term grades, random lookup by week number: array. A playlist where you constantly insert the next song after the current one: linked list. Say the cost you are buying: the array sells you $O(1)$ access and charges you for middle inserts. The list sells you inserts and charges you a walk.

Silent drill — cover the green model lines

1. Cost of A[i]?

Model. $O(1)$.

2. What is stored in a list node besides the value?

Model. A pointer (reference) to the next node.

3. Why is the tenth list node not O(1)?

Model. You must follow nine pointers from the head. There is no index jump.

4. Frequent middle inserts. Array or list?

Model. List, once you are at the position.

5. Random access by position. Array or list?

Model. Array.

DAY12

Stack and queue

Unit 2 · Algorithms and Data Structures · 120-minute coached session

10 minRecall, cold
25 minTeach from the board
25 minWork the example together
40 minSilent drill, then mark
20 minHomework set and traps
Leave able to
  • PUSH and POP
  • Enqueue and dequeue
  • Match undo to a stack and a printer to a queue
Trap that costs the mark

Dequeuing from the rear because it is ‘the end’. The front leaves. The rear arrives.

Ten-minute recall — yesterday, closed book

AskAccept
Array access cost?O(1) by index
List access?Walk from the head

Board notes

A stack is LIFO: last in, first out. PUSH adds on top. POP removes from the top. Undo, a pile of plates, function-call return.

A queue is FIFO: first in, first out. Enqueue at the rear. Dequeue from the front. With two pointers both operations are $O(1)$. A printer line, a ticket window.

Do not mix the verbs. A stack has one end that matters. A queue has two.

Worked example — do not skip the steps

Board. PUSH 10, PUSH 20, POP, PUSH 30. The stack from bottom to top is 10, then 30. A second POP returns 30. The 10 is still there.

Queue. Enqueue 76, 90, 77. Dequeue returns 76. The new front is 90. The rear is still 77 until another enqueue.

Silent drill — cover the green model lines

1. LIFO structure and its two verbs.

Model. Stack. PUSH and POP.

2. FIFO structure and its two verbs.

Model. Queue. Enqueue at the rear, dequeue from the front.

3. Undo is which structure?

Model. A stack.

4. A fair printer is which structure?

Model. A queue. First document printed is the first submitted.

5. After PUSH 1, PUSH 2, POP, what remains?

Model. 1, alone.
Tonight
Act it with books (stack) and a line of three classmates (queue). Say the verb as you move.

DAY13

Trees and graphs

Unit 2 · Algorithms and Data Structures · 120-minute coached session

10 minRecall, cold
25 minTeach from the board
25 minWork the example together
40 minSilent drill, then mark
20 minHomework set and traps
Leave able to
  • Define root, parent, and child
  • Define vertex and edge
  • Refuse a tree when roads loop
Trap that costs the mark

Calling every diagram with lines a tree. If there are two paths, it is a graph.

Ten-minute recall — yesterday, closed book

AskAccept
Stack removal verb?POP
Queue removal end?Front

Board notes

A tree is a hierarchy from one root. Each child has one parent. Family trees, organisation charts, folders. No cycles.
