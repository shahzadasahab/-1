0. The journey is the syllabus

An entrepreneur notices a problem and builds a product or service — sometimes from a phone, an Instagram catalogue, or a WhatsApp price list. Entrepreneurship is the practice of planning, starting, testing, and improving, with creativity, risk, and a wish to last. The digital age makes the first test cheap: forms, payments, social posts, paper screenshots.

Local idea sparks from the book: fee reminders, an Ajrak catalogue, test reminders, canteen pre-order, van-delay updates, a notes hub.

1. Prototype — a thing you can point at

A prototype is an early model that shows how the idea looks and might work. It does not have to function. Paper, chart paper, Canva, Figma, PowerPoint, phone screenshots all count — including in a low-resource classroom with no lab.

1.1 The build loop

Design
Who is it for? What one job?
Build
The cheapest thing that can be shown.
Test
Watch real people. Shut up while they try.
Iterate
Change the drawing. Repeat. This is not failure.

ClassBuddy / Homework Helper activity: interview $10$ students, draw paper screens, test with $5$ classmates, then fix the button that nobody could find, add a subject filter, add a weekly view. That sequence is the lesson.

2. MVP — the smallest thing that is actually used

A Minimum Viable Product has the fewest features that still let a real person complete the core job. A Google Form that takes canteen orders for three days is an MVP. A colour-perfect app that nobody has ordered from is not.

2.1 Riskiest assumption

Every idea sits on beliefs. The riskiest assumption is the belief that, if false, kills the idea. “Students will pay for tutoring” is riskier than “we can pick a blue theme”. Test the killer belief first, with an MVP, not with a logo.

2.2 Planning an MVP

Write: the problem, the first users, one core feature, the cheap tools, how you will tell those users it exists, how you will collect feedback, and what you will change next. Estimate a small cost (paper, mobile data, a canteen receipt book) — not a startup fantasy budget.

3. Case — canteen pre-order

1
Pain: the whole college arrives at once; the last row gets leftovers.
2
Paper prototype of a 5-item menu and a pickup slip.
3
MVP: Google Form (or a paper register). Orders close $20$ minutes before break. Separate pickup line.
4
Run three days. Count completed orders, no-shows, kitchen stress.
5
Iterate: drop the two dishes nobody wants; send a reminder; keep the menu tiny.

4. Beachhead market — start embarrassingly small

A beachhead is the first tiny market you can actually win. “All students in Pakistan” is a slogan. “Grade XII board-prep students in this college” is a beachhead. “One canteen, one break” is a beachhead. Win a beach, then hop to the next cove.

Table 3. Weak versus strong first market
Too broadFocused beachhead
Every student in the countryXII pre-board students, one campus
All city canteensOur canteen, 10:40 break

Other project seeds: notice organiser, lost-and-found, library-book checker, crafts order form. Stay ethical: permission, no scraped personal data, no fake “sold out” tricks, no spam to a class group.

Self-check

  1. A clickable Figma of a van-tracker with no live GPS — prototype or MVP?
  2. Name a riskiest assumption for a paid notes hub.
  3. Rewrite “an app for all Pakistani students” as a beachhead sentence.
Answers. (1) Prototype (it cannot yet be used for a real van). (2) “Students will pay rather than photocopy.” (3) e.g. “XII Computer Science students in this college who missed last week’s practical.”

Recap

  1. Entrepreneurship here is a loop, not a vocabulary test.
  2. Problem $\to$ idea $\to$ prototype $\to$ MVP $\to$ beachhead.
  3. Fidelity: low / mid / high. Paper is a legal prototype.
  4. Prototype asks “clear?”; MVP asks “used?”.
  5. Test the riskiest assumption first. Start with a market small enough to name in one breath.
  6. Collect feedback, change the thing, stay ethical.
