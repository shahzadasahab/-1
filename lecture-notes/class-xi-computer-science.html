Computer Systems

Unit 1

By the end of this unit you can separate analog from digital representation, build truth tables and logic diagrams, simplify small expressions with a Karnaugh map, explain why a software project follows a life cycle, compare Waterfall with Agile, and trace a message through the OSI and TCP/IP models.

1.1 Discrete quantities and continuous quantities

A discrete quantity is counted in separate steps. A continuous quantity can fall anywhere on a range. Computers that use logic gates live in the discrete world: each signal is treated as one of two symbols.

Discrete — stairs

Number of students, books on a shelf, fingers, bits in a byte. You can list the allowed values. There is no “2.5 students” in a headcount.

Continuous — ramp

Temperature, height, time on a clock, the speed of a car. Between any two readings there is another possible reading.

1.2 Analog and digital signals

An analog signal tracks a physical quantity smoothly. A microphone voltage that follows a voice is analog. A digital signal is sampled into symbols. In the circuits of this unit the symbols are only 0 and 1: LOW and HIGH, off and on, false and true.

Analog and digital, side by side.

Remember
A digital system is still built from the physical world. The representation is discrete. The wire voltage may be 0 V or 5 V, and the logic only cares which band it is in.

1.3 Boolean algebra

Boolean algebra is the arithmetic of those two symbols. A variable such as \(A\), \(B\), or \(Y\) is either 0 or 1. The three basic operations are AND, OR, and NOT. Several notations appear in books; this course uses the set below.

AND
\(Y = A \cdot B\). Also written \(AB\). Output is 1 only when every input is 1.
OR
\(Y = A + B\). Output is 1 when at least one input is 1.
NOT
\(Y = \overline{A}\). Also written \(A'\). Output is the opposite of the input.
Order
Parentheses, then NOT, then AND, then OR. So \(A + B \cdot C = A + (B \cdot C)\), and \(\overline{A + B}\) is not the same as \(\overline{A} + B\).

Worked evaluation. Let \(A = 1\), \(B = 1\), \(C = 1\), and \(Y = A \cdot B + \overline{C}\).

  1. NOT first: \(\overline{C} = \overline{1} = 0\).
  2. AND next: \(A \cdot B = 1 \cdot 1 = 1\).
  3. OR last: \(Y = 1 + 0 = 1\).

De Morgan’s laws let you push a bar through a gate and swap the operation. They are the reason a NAND can stand in for other gates.

De Morgan
\(\overline{A \cdot B} = \overline{A} + \overline{B}\) and \(\overline{A + B} = \overline{A} \cdot \overline{B}\).

1.7 Logic diagrams

Draw a gate for each operation, starting with the highest precedence. Wires carry the intermediate results. Do not let two output wires touch.

1.8 Karnaugh maps

A K-map is a truth table redrawn so that neighbours differ by exactly one variable. That neighbour rule is Gray code, not ordinary binary counting. For two variables the column order is \(\overline{B}, B\). For three variables the column order is \(BC = 00, 01, 11, 10\). You group adjacent 1s in blocks of size \(1, 2, 4, 8, \ldots\). Larger groups give simpler terms. Groups may overlap and may wrap around the edges. Each group becomes one product term; the variables that change inside the group are dropped.

Try
Fill a 2-variable map for XOR: outputs 0, 1, 1, 0. Explain why the two 1s cannot form a legal group. The simplified expression stays \(A\overline{B}+\overline{A}B\).

1.9 Building the same circuit in Logisim-evolution

Logisim-evolution is the simulation tool for this unit. The habit matters more than any one menu name.

  1. Install the Windows build, or the platform-independent JAR if you already have Java. Open a project and save it with a .circ name before you draw.
  2. From the explorer, open Gates and place a gate. The cursor shows a ghost shape until you click the canvas. Press Escape when you want the select tool back.
  3. Input pins are square and can be toggled. Output pins are round and show the result. Place inputs on the left and the output on the right. Double-click a pin to label it.
  4. Draw wires from pin to pin. In this tool, wires run horizontally or vertically. A blue wire has no defined value: an input is still floating. A grey wire is not connected at an end. Bright green is logic 1. Dark green is logic 0. Orange or red means a conflict, often two outputs tied together.
  5. Change the number of inputs, or the facing direction, from the attribute table while the gate is selected. Simulate and tick through every row of the truth table. For an AND gate the output is bright green only on the row 1, 1.
Lab check
Before you trust a circuit, no wire should be blue or grey. Then compare the pin states with a truth table you wrote by hand.

1.10 The software development life cycle

Software is not only source code. It includes the requirements, the design, the tests, the installed product, and the work of keeping it useful. The software development life cycle (SDLC) is the planned path through that work. Skipping a phase usually means paying for it later as confusion, rework, or a system nobody can maintain.

PhaseThe question it answersA school-system example
AnalysisWhat must the software do, and for whom?Register a book loan, a return, and an overdue notice.
DesignHow will data, screens, and modules be shaped?Tables for books and members; a loan screen; a flowchart of return.
ImplementationHow is the design turned into a working build?Code to the agreed names, comments, and layout.
TestingDoes it meet the requirements?Unit tests on a fine calculation; a full loan-and-return trial.
DeploymentHow does it reach the real users?Install on the library desk, train the librarian, keep a backup.
MaintenanceHow is it corrected, adapted, or improved?Fix a date bug; add a new report the principal requests.

Black-box testing

You know the required inputs and outputs, not the internal code. A librarian enters a member id and expects the right loan list. Good for acceptance checks.

White-box testing

You can see the code and you choose inputs that walk each branch. Good for the developer who must know that both sides of an if have run.

Deployment may be immediate, phased, or parallel with the old process until the new one is trusted. Maintenance is corrective (repair a defect), adaptive (meet a new rule or platform), perfective (make a used feature better), or preventive (reduce a future risk).

1.11 Waterfall and Agile

A life-cycle model is a way of moving through those phases. Waterfall walks them once, in order. A phase is meant to finish, and be signed off, before the next begins. Agile, and the Scrum form of it used in this course, repeats a short cycle so that working software and fresh feedback arrive often.

Waterfall fits when

  • The requirements are known and stable.
  • The product is like a library system: loans, returns, catalogue, fines.
  • A documented hand-off between phases is required.

Strength: a clear plan and an early design. Risk: a change discovered late is expensive, because earlier phases are treated as closed.

Agile fits when

  • Stakeholders disagree or discover needs as they try the software.
  • The product is like a school mobile app: timetable, results, notices, homework.
  • People need a usable slice in weeks, not a finished system in half a year.

Strength: change is expected. Risk: weak discipline turns “flexible” into “unfinished”.

Comparison you can defend with the two case studies: a library system whose rules were known, and a school app whose features kept shifting.
WaterfallAgile (Scrum)
RequirementsGathered up frontEmerge and get reordered
DeliveryOne release after the sequenceA working increment every sprint
Customer roleReviews documents at phase gatesTries software and speaks in the review
ChangeFormally expensiveExpected between sprints
Best evidenceStable rules, a schema, a test recordA backlog, sprint summaries, feedback that changed the next sprint

1.12 Communication models

A communication model is a shared picture of how a message moves from one device to another. It divides the job so that each layer can change without rewriting every other layer. The two models in this unit are OSI, which is a teaching and design reference with seven layers, and TCP/IP, which is the working model of the internet with four.

7ApplicationHTTP, HTTPS, FTP, SMTP, DNS
6Presentationformat, compression, encryption
5Sessionopen, manage, close a dialogue
4TransportTCP, UDP, ports
3NetworkIP, ICMP, routing
2Data linkMAC, frames, switches
1Physicalbits, cable, hub, radio

Bottom to top, layers 1 to 7: “Please Do Not Throw Sausage Pizza Away.”

Application, presentation, session

Layer 7 gives the program a network service. Layer 6 translates, compresses, and, in the OSI picture, encrypts. Layer 5 keeps a conversation in order: who is talking, and when the conversation ends. Protocols named with this layer include NetBIOS and RPC.

Transport and network

Layer 4 moves data between processes, using port numbers. Layer 3 moves packets between networks, using logical addresses. IPv4 addresses are 32 bits, as in 192.168.1.1. IPv6 addresses are 128 bits.

Data link and physical

Layer 2 frames the bits, uses MAC addresses, and detects errors on the local link. Its two sublayers are LLC and MAC. Switches and wireless access points live here. Layer 1 moves the raw signal. Hubs and cables do not understand addresses.

TCP and UDP share layer 4 and solve different problems.
TCPUDP
ConnectionSet up, then transferred, then closedNo handshake

Encapsulation

On the way down, each layer wraps the unit it received. On the way up, each layer strips the wrapper it understands. That wrapping is encapsulation; the reverse is decapsulation.

TCP/IP folds the OSI layers. Learn the fold, not a second unrelated stack.

Why both
OSI is the precise vocabulary for “which job is this?”. TCP/IP is the model that real internet devices implement. A good answer uses OSI names and then says where that job sits in TCP/IP.

Computational Thinking and Algorithms

Unit 2

By the end of this unit you can take a messy problem apart, name an algorithm’s properties, write the idea in steps and in pseudocode, trace bubble sort, selection sort, linear search, and binary search, and choose among them using correctness, efficiency, and clarity.

2.1 Four moves, then an algorithm

Computational thinking is a way of working on a problem so that a computer could later carry it out. The course uses four moves. An algorithm is the result of those moves: a finite list of precise steps that produces the required output from the allowed input, and that does not depend on any one programming language.

Decomposition

Split the problem until each piece can be solved, tested, and reused. Learning a language splits into vocabulary, word order, tense, sentences, listening, and feedback. A program splits the same way: input, process, output, and the errors between them.

Pattern recognition

Ask what repeats, what changes in a regular way, and what can be generalised. A triangle of stars grows by one star each row. That pattern is a loop, not twenty separate print statements.

Abstraction

Keep the steps that define the task. Park the details that do not. Making tea, in every kitchen, is: boil water, add tea, steep, pour, serve. Brand of kettle and colour of cup are noise. Sugar and milk are parameters, not a different algorithm.

Algorithm design

Write the remaining steps so a stranger can follow them. If two careful readers can disagree about the next action, the step is not definite yet.

Star rows
Row \(i\) has \(i\) stars. After \(n\) rows the total is \(\displaystyle \sum_{i=1}^{n} i = \frac{n(n+1)}{2}\). For \(n=4\), the picture uses \(10\) stars. Counting the pattern is already an algorithm.

2.2 Algorithm, pseudocode, and the properties of a good one

Algorithm

The logical procedure. Numbered steps or plain sentences. Written for a person who must understand the idea, including someone who does not program. No required keywords.

Pseudocode

The same procedure in a structured sketch: IF, ELSE, FOR, WHILE, and indentation. It is not executed. It is the bridge you cross just before real code. Keywords are conventional, not a secret syntax.

When a question asks for the properties of a good algorithm, list these five, then judge the result with the three criteria in section 2.6.

PropertyMeaning in practice
InputThe data the steps are allowed to see is stated. Zero inputs is allowed; an undeclared input is not.
OutputAt least one result is produced: a value, a rearranged list, or a clear “not found”.
DefinitenessEach step has one meaning. “Sort the names nicely” fails. “Swap if the left value is greater” holds.
FinitenessThe steps end. A loop needs a condition that becomes false for every legal input.
EffectivenessEach step is basic enough to do. “Find the best route” is a new problem. “Compare these two numbers” is a step.

2.3 Logical reasoning inside a step

An IF is a claim. From “if the remainder on division by 2 is 0, then the number is even” you may trust the contrapositive: if it is not even, then the remainder was not 0. You may not automatically trust the converse unless the definition runs both ways. In this definition it does, because even means exactly that remainder. In a school rule such as “if the fee is unpaid, then the slip is withheld”, the converse (“if the slip is withheld, then the fee is unpaid”) may be false. Algorithmic thinking includes noticing which direction you used.

2.4 Bubble sort

Bubble sort, also called ripple sort, walks the list again and again, comparing neighbours and swapping them when they are out of order. After pass \(k\), the \(k\) largest items that must bubble toward the end have arrived there. It sorts in place: the swaps happen inside the original list, so the extra memory is a single temporary variable. Equal items keep their original order if you swap only when the left one is strictly greater, so this version is stable.

DATA = [6, 3, 8, 2]
N = 4
for pass = 1 to N - 1
    for i = 1 to N - pass
        if DATA[i] > DATA[i + 1] then
            swap DATA[i] with DATA[i + 1]
        end if
    end for
end for

Start

6382

After pass 1

3628

8 has reached the end.

After pass 2

3268

After pass 3

2368

Pass 1 comparisons: \(6>3\) swap, \(6>8\) keep, \(8>2\) swap, leaving 3, 6, 2, 8. Pass 2: \(3>6\) keep, \(6>2\) swap. Pass 3: \(3>2\) swap. The list is 2, 3, 6, 8.

Cost
Neighbours compared, whether or not they swap: \(\dfrac{n(n-1)}{2}\). For \(n=4\) that is 6. Double the list and the comparisons grow about fourfold, which is why we write the time as \(O(n^2)\). Fine for a classroom list. Poor for a large register.

Why teach it

The picture is “two cards in the wrong order”. You can trace it on paper. It needs no second copy of the list.

Why not stop there

Every swap is three assignments. The nested loops do a lot of work even when the list is nearly ordered, unless you add an early-exit flag. Large data wants a faster sort.

2.5 Selection sort

Selection sort also uses nested passes, but it swaps less often. On pass \(k\) it finds the smallest item in the still-unsorted tail and swaps that item into position \(k\).

for i = 1 to N - 1
    minPos = i
    for j = i + 1 to N
        if DATA[j] < DATA[minPos] then
            minPos = j
        end if
    end for
    swap DATA[i] with DATA[minPos]
end for

On 6, 3, 8, 2 the minimum of the whole list is 2, so pass 1 swaps it with 6 and leaves 2, 3, 8, 6. Pass 2 looks at 3, 8, 6 and finds that 3 is already home. Pass 3 swaps 8 and 6. Result: 2, 3, 6, 8. Comparisons are still about \(n(n-1)/2\), so the time class is still \(O(n^2)\), but the number of swaps is at most \(n-1\). That matters when moving an item is expensive. Selection sort is not stable in the simple form above: an earlier equal value can be moved past a later one when the minimum is swapped into place.

2.6 Linear search

Linear search, or sequential search, looks at position 1, then 2, then 3, until it finds the target or finishes the list. The list may be unsorted. If the target sits at the end, or is absent, every element is examined.

found = false
position = 0
i = 1
while i <= N and found = false
    if DATA[i] = target then
        found = true
        position = i
    else
        i = i + 1
    end if
end while

Search for 8 in 6, 3, 8, 2. Position 1 is 6, position 2 is 3, position 3 is 8. Stop. Three comparisons. Worst case comparisons: \(n\), written \(O(n)\). Doubling the list doubles the worst-case wait.

2.7 Binary search

Binary search is allowed only on a sorted list, stored so you can jump to the middle index. Arrays allow that. A pile of unsorted forms does not, until you sort it. Each step keeps the half that can still contain the target.

Middle
\(\mathrm{mid} = \mathrm{INT}\!\left(\dfrac{\mathrm{beg}+\mathrm{end}}{2}\right)\). INT means the whole-number part. List positions are whole numbers, so \(\mathrm{INT}(3.5)=3\).
beg = 1
end = N
loc = 0
while beg <= end and loc = 0
    mid = INT((beg + end) / 2)
    if DATA[mid] = target then
        loc = mid
    else if target < DATA[mid] then
        end = mid - 1
    else
        beg = mid + 1
    end if
end while

Sorted list 2, 3, 6, 8. Target 6. First middle is \(\mathrm{INT}((1+4)/2)=2\), and position 2 holds 3. The target is greater, so beg becomes 3. Next middle is \(\mathrm{INT}((3+4)/2)=3\), and position 3 holds 6. Found.

Cost
Worst-case probes are about \(\lfloor \log_2 n \rfloor + 1\), written \(O(\log n)\). The price you pay first is sorting, plus careful index updates. An off-by-one on beg or end can loop forever or skip the target. Linear search is harder to get wrong.

2.9 Correctness, efficiency, clarity

These are the evaluation criteria. A clever algorithm that fails one of them is not finished.

Correctness

For every allowed input, the output matches the definition. Include empty lists, one item, duplicates, and values that are not present. The procedure must end.

Efficiency

Time: how the step count grows with \(n\). Space: extra memory beyond the input. Linear search is the right answer on a short unsorted list and the wrong answer on a million sorted records searched all day.

Clarity

Another student can trace it, name the variables, and change it without guessing. Pseudocode that hides a step inside “and so on” fails clarity even if the idea is correct.

A correctness trace. Input a whole number. If the remainder on division by 2 is 0, say even; otherwise say odd.

The row for 0 is the one students skip. Zero is even. A trace that never leaves the happy path has not shown correctness.

3.1 Languages, and why this course uses Python

Machine language

Bits the processor executes. Exact, and painful to write by hand.

Assembly

Short mnemonics for those instructions. Still tied to one family of processors.

High-level

Closer to the problem than to the chip. Python sits here. An interpreter runs it, so you can edit and try a program quickly.

The same language shows up in software work, data analysis, machine learning, web services, automation, and security tooling. The reason it is the teaching language is narrower than the job list: the syntax is small, the feedback is immediate, and the ideas of Unit 2 (variables, conditions, loops) stay visible.

3.6 Control: sequence, selection, repetition

Every program in this unit is a mix of three structures. Sequence means the lines run from top to bottom. Selection means a condition chooses a branch. Repetition means a block runs while a condition holds, or once for each item of a sequence.

age = int(input("Age: "))
if age <= 0:
    print("Invalid age")
elif age <= 12:
    print("Child")
elif age <= 17:
    print("Teenager")
elif age <= 59:
    print("Adult")
else:
    print("Senior")

Conditions are tried from the top. The first true branch wins, and the later ones are skipped. An if without else simply does nothing when the condition is false. Use else when every input must produce one of two messages. Nest an if only when the second question matters after the first has been answered, for example a fee check that runs only once age has already qualified.

for and range

range(5) yields 0, 1, 2, 3, 4. The stop value is excluded. range(2, 8) starts at 2. range(0, 10, 2) steps by 2. A negative step counts down: range(10, 0, -2) yields 10, 8, 6, 4, 2.

while

Use while when you cannot know the trip count in advance: read items until the user types done. Something inside the loop must change the condition. If nothing does, the program does not finish.

total = 0.0
count = 0
while True:
    item = input("Item, or done: ")
    if item.lower() == "done":
        break
    price = float(input("Price: "))
    if price <= 0:
        print("Price must be positive")
        continue
    total += price
    count += 1
print(count, f"{total:.2f}")

break leaves the loop. continue skips the rest of this trip and starts the next test. A nested loop is a loop in the body of a loop. The inner one finishes its whole run before the outer one takes its next step. A 3 by 3 table of products is the clean demonstration:

for i in range(1, 4):
    for j in range(1, 4):
        print(i * j, end=" ")
    print()

3.7 A report-card calculation

Three subjects, each out of 100. Percentage is a ratio, then a scale.

Percentage
\(\displaystyle P = \frac{m_1+m_2+m_3}{300}\times 100\). Pass if \(P \ge 50\). Change 50 only when the rule changes, and change it in one place.
m1 = float(input("Mathematics: "))
m2 = float(input("Science: "))
m3 = float(input("Computer: "))
total = m1 + m2 + m3
percentage = (total / 300) * 100
status = "PASS" if percentage >= 50 else "FAIL"
print(f"Total {total:.0f}   {percentage:.2f}%   {status}")

3.8 Libraries

A library is code someone else has already tested. You import it instead of pasting it.

  • import math then math.sqrt(81), math.floor, math.ceil, math.pi.
  • import math as m shortens the prefix.
  • from math import sqrt lets you call sqrt with no prefix. Importing with * dumps every name into your file and makes clashes likely. Prefer the named form.
  • random.randint(1, 10) includes both ends. random.random() is a float from 0 up to but not including 1.

A third-party library is installed into the environment, typically with pip, and only then imported. A built-in library such as math needs no install. If import fails, the message is almost always “not installed” or “misspelled”, not a bug in your arithmetic.

3.9 When the program stops

A traceback is a stack of calls. Read the last line first: that is the error type and the short reason. Above it, the file name and line number point at your statement. A syntax error means Python could not even start, often a missing colon, a bad indent, or = in a condition. A runtime error means the program started and then met an impossible operation.

ValueError: invalid literal for int() with base 10: 'abc'

That line came from int("abc") or from a user who typed letters into a numeric input. It does not mean every line above it is nonsense. An IndentationError means a block is not aligned with the header that owns it. Fix the reported line, run again, and deal with the next message. Guessing at distant lines creates new faults.

Data and Analysis

Unit 4

By the end of this unit you can separate data from information, name the parts of a database, choose keys, draw an entity-relationship diagram, protect referential integrity, and create a table, a form, and a query in Microsoft Access, including a grouped summary and a chart.

4.1 Data, information, and a DBMS

Data

Recorded facts that have not yet been given a purpose: 17, “Inaya”, “IS-101”, a date. On their own they do not tell you what to do.

Information

Data arranged so a person can decide. “Inaya is enrolled in IS-101 and has attended 17 of 20 classes” is information. The same digits, with a context and a question, changed jobs.

A database is an organised collection of related data. A database management system (DBMS) is the software that stores it and lets you define structure, insert and edit rows, ask questions, and produce reports, without each program inventing its own files. The advantages you should be able to list are less repeated data, consistency of shared facts, controlled access, and queries that survive after the person who typed the data has left.

4.2 The pieces, from small to large

4.3 Keys and integrity

KeyWhat it promisesExample
Candidate keyA field, or a combination, that could uniquely identify a rowStudentID, or a national identity number if it is unique and always present
Primary keyThe candidate you actually chose. No duplicates, no empty valueStudentID
Composite keyA primary key made of more than one fieldStudentID + CourseID in a small enrolment table
Foreign keyA field whose values must match a primary key in a parent tableEnrolment.StudentID pointing at Student.StudentID

Referential integrity is the rule behind the foreign key: every child value either matches a living parent or is deliberately empty, if empty is allowed. You cannot enrol student 999 if no student 999 exists. Cascade update rewrites the child when the parent key changes. Cascade delete removes the children when the parent row is removed, so a departed student does not leave orphan enrolments. Use cascade delete only when the child has no meaning without the parent. A book should not vanish because one borrower record was deleted.

4.4 The entity-relationship model

An ER model draws the world before you open Access. An entity is a rectangle: a type of thing, such as Student or Book. An attribute is an oval: one fact about that thing. A relationship is a diamond: a verb that connects entities. Underline the primary-key attribute.

Cardinality says how many. One-to-one: a person and that person’s current student card. One-to-many: one member borrows many records over the year, and each borrow record belongs to one member. Many-to-many: one member borrows many books, and one book is borrowed by many members over time. A relational schema cannot store a many-to-many link as a single foreign key. You resolve it with a new entity, the borrow record, and two one-to-many relationships.

The same steps work for any case study. Write the requirements in sentences. Underline the nouns: those are candidate entities. List attributes, mark primary keys, name the verbs, fix the cardinality, and only then resolve every many-to-many. A library needs at least Member (MemberID, name, contact), Book (BookID, title, author, publisher), and Borrow (BorrowID, MemberID, BookID, date out, date due). Author can stay an attribute while each book has one author string. It becomes an entity the day you must list every book by one author without retyping the name.

4.5 Objects you will meet in Access

Table

The stored rows. Design the table before you decorate anything else. A pretty form on a bad table preserves the bad table.

Form

A screen for one job: add a student, edit a phone number, avoid typing into the raw grid. Controls include text boxes, combo boxes, radio buttons, check boxes, dates, and command buttons.

Query

A question saved in the database: which students, which department, what average. Queries read tables. They are how analysis starts.

Report and chart

A layout for a reader who will not open the grid. Bar, column, and line charts belong here when the pattern is easier to see than the numbers.

4.6 Building a table

Plan on paper first: field name, data type, size, which field is the primary key, and which values are required. Then choose a view.

Datasheet view

Create → Table. You type into a grid, as in a spreadsheet. Access invents Field1, Field2 until you rename the headers. Fast for a sketch. Weak when you need validation rules, formats, and a carefully chosen key.

Design view

Create → Table Design. Each row of the designer is one field: name, data type, optional description. Select the key field and set Primary Key. This is the view to use once the plan exists.

A form needs a record source: the table or query whose fields it edits. The Form Wizard asks which fields, which layout, and which title. Move StudentID, name, department, and contact across. 4.8 Queries, filters, and summaries

Create → Query Design, add the Student table, and double-click the fields you want to see. A criterion under a column restricts rows. Text criteria are written in quotes.

One condition

Under Department, the criterion "CS" returns only that department. Under a mark column, >= 50 keeps the passes.

AND

Two criteria on the same Criteria row must both be true: department CS and mark at least 50.

OR

The second condition goes on the or row, or you write "CS" Or "IT" in one cell. Either department qualifies.

A totals query groups rows. Open the Totals row. Group By the department. Use Count on the student id, Avg on the mark, Sum when the column is a quantity that should add, Min and Max when you need the extremes. The result has one row per department, which is the point: you have stopped listing students and started describing groups.

Average
\(\displaystyle \bar{x} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i\). Access computes it inside the group you named. An average of all students in the college, and an average per department, answer different questions. The Group By column decides which one you asked.

Charts: a bar or column chart compares categories, such as average mark by department. A line chart follows a measure across time, such as loans per month. A stacked column shows parts inside a total, such as lectures by teacher inside one subject. Pick the chart from the question. A line through unrelated departments implies a time order that is not there.

Applications and Impacts of Computing

Unit 5

By the end of this unit you can describe how AI, the Internet of Things, data analytics, and cloud services fit together, give a concrete use in cities, industry, health, education, energy, farming, and wearables, judge whether a source is primary, secondary, or tertiary, and argue a positive impact beside a negative one, including the role of assistive technology.

5.1 One pipeline, four names

A smart system is easier to remember as a pipeline than as a list of slogans. Devices sense the world. A network carries the readings. Storage, often on a cloud service, holds them. Analytics looks for a pattern. AI uses that pattern to choose an action. A person remains responsible for the choice when the action affects someone.

Artificial intelligence

“Artificial” means people built it. “Intelligence” here means a task that used to need a judgement: recognising a face to unlock a phone, ranking videos, answering a routine question. The system is using data, not understanding a room the way a person does. Treat the output as a proposal.

Internet of Things

Ordinary objects with a sensor or actuator, an address, and a way to exchange data: watches, meters, cameras, pumps, vehicles. Common links include Wi-Fi, mobile data, and short-range radio. The object can report, and sometimes act, without a person typing the reading.

Data analytics

Collect, clean, group, and interpret. A teacher looking at a mark list to see which topic failed is doing analytics with a small set. A city doing the same with traffic sensors is the same idea at another scale. Sources include sensors, forms, transaction logs, and public records.

Cloud computing

Storage and software reached over the internet, rather than only on the device in your hand. Drive-style storage, a learning-management system, and shared classroom tools are the examples to cite. IoT uses the cloud because the devices themselves are too small to keep years of readings.

Careers sit on this pipeline: sensor technician, network installer, data analyst, junior AI developer, farm-technology adviser, health-informatics assistant. The skills that transfer are careful data collection, basic networking, a spreadsheet or a Python script, and the habit of asking what the device does when the link fails.

5.3 Information sources, and when they stop being reliable

Unit 6 will collect data. This unit classifies the sources you read.

PrimarySecondaryTertiary

An official identity register is a primary source for the facts it was created to hold. It is not a primary source for a rumour someone typed into a comment underneath a news story. A textbook is secondary: useful for learning, and the wrong citation if the question was “what did the agency itself publish?”. Reliability is not a badge that sticks forever. Check who produced it, when, for what purpose, and whether you can see the underlying record.

The matching skills are library and archive work, fact-checking, research assistance, and science communication. Each of them is mostly the discipline of saying where a claim came from.

5.4 Impacts, including assistive technology

Gains you can evidence

  • A bank customer reaches an account without travelling to a branch.
  • A student replays a lesson; a clinic keeps a record that another shift can read.
  • A farmer skips a wasted irrigation cycle.

Costs you must also name

  • Dependence: when the system stops, the manual skill has already faded.
  • Privacy: a wearable and a school camera collect more than the user notices.
  • Uneven access: a service that exists only on a new phone excludes people.
  • Unchecked automation: a wrong sensor still triggers a confident action.

Assistive technology is the family of tools that reduce a barrier: mobility aids, screen readers and captions, hearing support, communication devices, and accessible interfaces in classrooms and hospitals. The purpose is participation. A student who can hear the lesson through captions, or read it through a screen reader, is using computing as access rather than as convenience. Careers in this area need both the technical skill and the habit of testing with the person who will rely on the tool.

A fair paragraph
One positive, one negative, and the condition that decides which one you are living in. “Mobile banking saves a journey; it also fails the customer who has no signal and no alternative counter.” That is an analytical answer. A list of brand names is not.

Digital Literacy

Unit 6

By the end of this unit you can run a precise search, design a way to collect original data, tell primary data from secondary data, present both with a suitable tool, and publish a digital artefact that cites its sources and treats respondents fairly.

6.1 Literacy, not just access

Digital literacy is the ability to find, judge, shape, and share information with digital tools, and to do it safely. Owning a phone is not the skill. The skill is knowing which query to type, which result to trust, how to collect a fact you cannot look up, and how to show your work so someone else can check it.

The information age is the condition in which that skill matters: a large volume of data is published continuously, and access is no longer the scarce part. Judgement is.

6.2 Qualitative and quantitative data

Qualitative

Words about experience, reason, and meaning. “I revise with recorded lectures because the classroom is noisy.” You group comments; you do not average them.

Quantitative

Numbers you can count or measure. Hours online, a score out of 10, a yes that you later total. You can compute a mean only after the question was asked as a number.

QualitativeQuantitative
Question shapeHow, why, what is it likeHow many, how often, how much
Typical toolInterview notes, open responsesCounts, scales, spreadsheet columns
WeaknessHarder to compare fairlyMisses the reason behind the number

A strong class inquiry uses both. “Average phone use is 3.5 hours” is incomplete until a comment explains whether those hours were lectures or a feed.

6.3 Ways of collecting

Interview

Few people, deep answers, a prepared guide, permission to note or record. Good for reasons. Poor for claiming “most students”.

Survey

The same questions put to many people, on paper or in a form tool. Good for counts. Weak if the questions nudge the answer.

Observation

Watch a real activity and record an agreed checklist. Good for what people do. Weak if they change behaviour because they are watched.

Prototype

A small version of a design, tested with users. The data is what failed in the trial, not a speech about the idea.

Simulation

A model of a system you cannot safely or cheaply run, such as a queue at a lab or a spread of a rumour through a class network. Say what the model assumes. A simulation reports the model, not the world, until you compare it with a real measurement.

6.4 Primary data and secondary data

This distinction is about who collected it, which is not the same phrase as the primary and secondary sources of Unit 5, though they often line up.

Primary dataSecondary data
DefinitionCollected by you for this question: your survey, interview, observation, or experimentCollected by someone else, then reused: a report, a book, a published table, a database
AdvantageFits your question and your classWider than one classroom; already cleaned
RiskA small or biased sampleA different definition, an old year, or a hidden agenda
Do not swap them
A government PDF you downloaded is secondary data for your project, even if it was primary work for the agency that gathered it. Your classmate’s survey responses are primary data for you. Copying those numbers out of a website does not make them primary.

6.5 Designing the approach

Write these down before you collect anything. A project that starts with a form and invents the question afterwards cannot be repaired by a better chart.

  1. Question. One sentence a stranger can answer with yes, no, or a measurement. “How does internet use affect weekday study time in this Class XI section?” can be studied. “Is the internet good?” cannot.
  2. What you need. Mark each fact as qualitative or quantitative, primary or secondary.
  3. Who. The population, then the people you will actually reach. A convenience sample of friends is allowed in class if you name it as convenience and refuse to generalise to the country.
  4. Method and tool. Survey, interview, observation, prototype, or simulation, plus the form, sheet, or notebook.
  5. Ethics. Ask permission. Collect only what the question needs. Do not publish names beside sensitive answers. Let a respondent stop.
  6. Analysis plan. Which total, which average, which groups of comments, which chart.

A handful of results is a failure if they are the wrong handful. Operators narrow the pile.

6.7 Presenting

Spreadsheet

The working table: raw responses, then calculated columns. Average, count, and a chart that uses one question. Keep the raw sheet. A chart without the table cannot be checked.

Slides

One claim per slide, a chart that supports it, and very little prose. The talk carries the sentences the slide does not.

Infographic

A single page for a reader who will not study the sheet. Few numbers, each one labelled with its unit and its sample size.

Report

The full argument: question, method, results, limits, sources. This is the artefact a teacher can mark against the outcomes.

From a sheet
If five students report 2, 3, 3, 4, and 3 hours, then \(n=5\) and \(\bar{x} = (2+3+3+4+3)/5 = 3\). State \(n\) beside every average. An average of three willing friends is not an average of the section.

6.8 A worked inquiry

Question: in this section, how is weekday phone use split between study and distraction? Secondary search first, with a quoted phrase and filetype:pdf, so the introduction is not only opinion. Then a short survey of the section.

Each item has one job. Mixing a reason into a yes/no question makes the column useless later.
ItemKind
On a weekday, how many hours is the phone on for study?Quantitative
How many hours is it on for something else?Quantitative

Enter the numbers in a sheet. Compute the two averages and the count of each “opened most” choice. Make two visuals only: a bar chart of the activities, and a comparison of the two hour-averages. In the comments, group the helping remarks and the distracting remarks rather than pasting every sentence. Put one secondary finding next to yours and say whether it agrees. End with a recommendation the section can actually try, and a sentence on the limit of the sample.

6.9 The artefact, the credit, the limit

Whatever form you hand in, the reader should find, in this order: title, research question, short introduction, method, primary findings, secondary summary, the charts, a conclusion, a recommendation, and references. Decoration that does not carry one of those jobs makes the artefact worse.

Plagiarism is presenting someone else’s words, images, numbers, or structure as if you made them. A citation is the repair for honest reuse: author, title, year, and where you found it, in the format your teacher set. Paraphrase still needs a citation, because the finding was not yours. Ethics of collection and ethics of credit are the same habit: do not take from a person, or from an author, without saying so.

Formula strip for the last week

Rows
\(2^n\) combinations in a truth table of \(n\) inputs.
Order
Parentheses, NOT, AND, OR. \(\overline{A\cdot B}=\overline{A}+\overline{B}\).
XOR
\(A\oplus B = A\overline{B}+\overline{A}B\).
Sort
Neighbour comparisons in the simple bubble sort: \(\dfrac{n(n-1)}{2}\), time \(O(n^2)\).
Search
Linear worst case \(n\). Binary, on a sorted list: \(\mathrm{mid}=\mathrm{INT}((\mathrm{beg}+\mathrm{end})/2)\), about \(\lfloor\log_2 n\rfloor+1\) probes.
Shifts
\(a\ll k = a\times 2^k\) and \(\sim a = -a-1\) in two’s complement.
Percent
\(P = (\text{total}/\text{maximum})\times 100\).
Mean
\(\bar{x} = \frac{1}{n}\sum x_i\), and \(n\) is part of the answer.
